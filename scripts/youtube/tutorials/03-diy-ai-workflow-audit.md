## Why this audit exists (0:20–1:00)

> Quick context. Most AI projects fail because they pick the wrong workflow to automate first. They pick something flashy — a customer-facing chatbot, a marketing copy generator — instead of something boring.
>
> The boring ones win. Always. Because the boring ones are *repetitive*, *high-volume*, and *low-judgment*. Those are the three traits AI handles brilliantly. We're going to find yours.

## Step 1 — List every workflow your team does weekly (1:00–2:30)

> **Step one. Grab a notebook. Spend 10 minutes listing every workflow your team handles in a typical week.**
>
> Don't filter. Just list. Examples:
>
> - Responding to lead emails.
> - Drafting client proposals.
> - Pulling data out of invoices to update QuickBooks.
> - Scheduling appointments.
> - Sending follow-ups to past customers.
> - Tagging support tickets.
> - Drafting social media posts.
> - Reviewing job applications.
>
> You want at least 15 items. Try for 20. The bad ones make the good ones look better.

**B-roll:** hand-write a real list on a notepad (top-down shot, doesn't need to be your handwriting if you'd rather use stock).

---

## Step 2 — Score each workflow on three axes (2:30–4:30)

> **Step two. For each workflow, score it 1 to 5 on three axes.**
>
> Axis one — **Frequency.** How often does this happen? Daily is a 5, weekly is a 3, monthly is a 1.
>
> Axis two — **Time cost.** How long does it take, summed across all team members, in a typical week? Over 10 hours a week is a 5. Under 1 hour is a 1.
>
> Axis three — **Judgment required.** How much human judgment does it need? A 1 means anyone could do it with a clear rulebook. A 5 means it requires real expertise and context.
>
> Now — and this is the key — for AI, you want **high frequency, high time cost, *low* judgment.** That third axis is inverted.
>
> So we compute an AI fit score: **Frequency + Time cost + (6 minus Judgment).**
>
> Highest scores rise to the top.

## Step 3 — Sanity-check the top 3 (4:30–6:00)

> **Step three. Take your top three. Run them through four quick sanity checks.**
>
> One: **does the work happen in software your AI can read?** If the workflow is "Bob picks up phone, talks to customer, scribbles on a sticky note" — no software, no AI. But if it's "lead emails come into Gmail, sit there until someone replies" — yes, that's automatable.
>
> Two: **is there a clear definition of done?** A reply was sent. An invoice was logged. A ticket was tagged. If you can't articulate when the workflow is finished, AI can't either.
>
> Three: **are the failure modes acceptable?** What happens if the AI gets it wrong 5 percent of the time? If a wrong invoice categorization is annoying but recoverable — fine. If a wrong proposal sent to a client kills the deal — you need a human review step.
>
> Four: **is the data clean enough?** AI doesn't work on chaos. If your customer records are in 6 different places with typos and inconsistent formats — you have a data project before you have an AI project.
>
> Anything that passes all four sanity checks is a real candidate.

---

## Step 4 — Estimate ROI (6:00–7:30)

> **Step four. Estimate ROI on the surviving candidates.**
>
> The math is dirt simple.
>
> **Hours saved per week × loaded hourly cost × 52 weeks = annual savings.**
>
> Take a workflow that costs your team 8 hours a week. Your loaded cost — salary plus benefits — is, say, $40 an hour. That's $320 a week, or about **$16,000 a year**.
>
> Now compare to build cost. For a focused AI automation, **$5,000 to $15,000** is a realistic range. Ongoing cost — API calls, monitoring — runs maybe $50 to $300 a month depending on volume.
>
> So you're looking at a 6-to-12-month payback on a workflow you'd save 8 hours a week on. After that — pure savings.
>
> If a candidate doesn't pencil out at a 12-month payback, drop it. Move to the next one.

## Step 5 — Write the brief (7:30–8:30)

> **Step five. Write a one-page brief on your top candidate.**
>
> Five sections:
>
> 1. **The workflow.** What is it, step by step.
> 2. **The inputs.** What data, in what system, in what format.
> 3. **The outputs.** What does success look like — what's the AI handing back.
> 4. **The failure mode plan.** When the AI is unsure, what happens. Human review? Flag for later? Skip?
> 5. **The metrics.** How will you know in 60 days whether it worked.
>
> One page. Don't overcomplicate it.
>
> That brief is what you bring to whoever builds the automation. Whether that's me, your in-house dev, or a freelancer — the brief saves them 4 hours of discovery and saves you 4 hours of "wait, what did we decide?"
