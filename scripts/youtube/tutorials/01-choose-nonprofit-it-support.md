## Segment 1 — Defining "IT support" first (0:20–1:30)

> Quick definition, because "IT support" is one of those phrases that means different things to different people.
>
> For a small nonprofit, IT support is usually five things:
>
> 1. Keeping your website online and secure.
> 2. Keeping staff laptops working — passwords, email, printer issues.
> 3. Keeping donor data safe — backups, access control.
> 4. Patching security holes before they get exploited.
> 5. Being on the other end of the phone when something inevitably breaks.
>
> If you're a larger nonprofit — 20+ staff, multiple programs, compliance requirements — IT support also covers user provisioning, license management, networking, and strategic planning. We'll come back to that.
>
> With that out of the way: your three options.

## Segment 2 — Option 1: Hiring in-house (1:30–3:30)

> **Option one: hiring an in-house IT person.**
>
> This is the option boards default to because it feels safe. "We'll just hire someone." Here's what it actually costs.
>
> A competent IT generalist makes between **$70,000 and $130,000** in salary depending on geography and seniority. Add 25 to 30 percent for benefits — health insurance, retirement match, payroll taxes. So the fully-loaded cost is **$90K to $170K a year.**
>
> For a small nonprofit, that's one or two program staff worth of payroll. That's a real tradeoff.
>
> When does this make sense?
>
> - You have constant tech needs — multiple offices, hundreds of users.
> - You have regulated data — HIPAA, FERPA — that requires hands-on management.
> - You have custom applications a vendor can't support.
> - You have the budget to support a full-time hire without sacrificing program work.
>
> When does it *not* make sense?
>
> - When that hire becomes a single point of failure. They take vacation. They leave. The work stops.
> - When their actual workload is 10 hours a week. They get bored. They leave. Same outcome.
> - When you can't compete with private-sector salaries. Then you hire someone less experienced, and they're learning on your donor data.
>
> For most 5-to-50 person nonprofits, in-house IT is overkill. Move on.

## Segment 3 — Option 2: A managed service provider (3:30–6:00)

> **Option two: a managed service provider, or MSP.**
>
> An MSP is a company that handles your IT under a predictable monthly fee. They cover helpdesk, security, monitoring, backups, often strategic planning — all bundled.
>
> What does it cost?
>
> Typical MSP pricing for a small-to-mid nonprofit is **$1,500 to $10,000 per month**, depending on staff count and scope. Verified 501(c)(3) MSPs — including us at CSuite Code — usually discount that.
>
> When does an MSP make sense?
>
> - You need consistent IT coverage but cannot justify a full-time hire.
> - You want one partner handling helpdesk, security, hosting, and maybe AI — instead of three vendors.
> - You value **predictable** budgeting. Same number every month.
>
> Here's what to *watch out for* with MSPs:
>
> - **Multi-year contracts.** Enterprise MSPs love three-year deals. For a nonprofit, that's a red flag — your funding situation can change overnight. Insist on month-to-month or annual.
> - **Account managers between you and the work.** If you're not allowed to talk directly to the engineer fixing your problem, you're paying for layers.
> - **Minimums sized for hospitals, not nonprofits.** If the MSP's *minimum* is $5K a month, you're not their target customer. Find one that's actually built for your size.
> - **No nonprofit pricing.** If they don't have a verified 501(c)(3) discount tier, they don't really work with nonprofits — they tolerate them.
>
> An MSP that's actually built for nonprofits will check all four of those boxes.

## Segment 4 — Option 3: Hourly tech support (6:00–7:00)

> **Option three: hourly tech support.**
>
> A freelancer or local IT shop you call when something breaks. They bill by the hour — typically **$100 to $250 per hour**, sometimes discounted to **$90 to $150** for verified nonprofits.
>
> When does hourly make sense?
>
> - You need help maybe a few times a year. A new staff laptop. A one-time migration. A donor page fix.
> - You're a very small org with very basic needs — a website that mostly works and three people on Google Workspace.
>
> When does it *not* make sense?
>
> - When your team interacts with technology every day. The math breaks down fast. Twenty hours a month at $150 an hour is $3,000 — and you've gotten zero security monitoring, zero proactive patching, zero backup verification.
> - When something breaks at 9 p.m. on a Saturday before a major event. Your hourly freelancer is not on call.
>
> Hourly is the right answer if you genuinely only need occasional help. It is the *wrong* answer if you're using it as a way to avoid committing to a monthly plan.

## Segment 5 — The decision framework (7:00–9:00)

> So how do you decide?
>
> Here's the simple framework I give every nonprofit leader who asks.
>
> **Question one: how many hours per month does your team interact with technology in a way that requires support?**
>
> - Under 5 hours: hourly is fine.
> - 5 to 40 hours: an MSP is the right answer.
> - 40+ hours, or 20+ staff, or regulated data: you might need an in-house person *plus* an MSP. (Yes, those two work together. The internal person handles day-to-day, the MSP handles after-hours and strategic.)
>
> **Question two: what's your annual operating budget?**
>
> A reasonable IT spend for a nonprofit is **2 to 5 percent of operating budget**, including software, hosting, hardware, and external support. For a $500,000 org, that's $10K to $25K a year — comfortably in MSP range. For a $2M org, that's $40K to $100K — you can afford an MSP plus serious software investment.
>
> **Question three: can you tolerate variance in your monthly tech bill?**
>
> If yes, hourly. If no, MSP. If you literally cannot have your website go down for a week, MSP — full stop.
>
> **Question four: how important is having one partner who knows your whole stack?**
>
> If you're tired of explaining your nonprofit to a new vendor every six months — pick an MSP that's built for nonprofits, and stick with them.

## Segment 6 — Wrap up (9:00–10:00)

> One more thing. Whoever you pick, ask these questions on the first call:
>
> - Do you offer verified 501(c)(3) pricing? How is "verified" handled?
> - Will I talk to the person doing the work?
> - Is the engagement month-to-month?
> - Do you understand grants, restricted funds, board reporting?
> - What's your first-response time guarantee?
> - Can you provide a reference from a similar-sized nonprofit?
>
> If they can't answer those clearly — keep looking.
