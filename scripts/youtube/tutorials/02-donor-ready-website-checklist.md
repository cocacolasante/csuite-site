## Item 1 — A donation button in the persistent nav (0:20–1:20)

> **Number one. Your donate button has to live in the persistent navigation.**
>
> That means: in the header, visible on every page, on every device, without scrolling.
>
> Why does this matter? Because donors don't always arrive on your homepage. They land on a program page, a news article, a board member's bio — wherever Google sent them. If they decide to donate in *that* moment, you have about 4 seconds before the impulse fades.
>
> If they have to scroll, click "About," find a sub-page, then locate a button — you've lost half of them.
>
> Make the donate button visually distinct — different color, slight outline, the verb "Donate" or "Give" written clearly. Not "Support Us." Not "Get Involved." **Donate.**

## Item 2 — A donation page that loads in under 2 seconds (1:20–2:20)

> **Number two. Your donation page has to load in under 2 seconds.**
>
> Google publishes the math on this. Every additional second of load time drops conversion rates by 7 percent. A donation page that loads in 5 seconds is leaving roughly 20 percent of donations on the table.
>
> How do you check? Run your page through PageSpeed Insights — it's a free Google tool. If your score is below 80 on mobile, you have work to do.
>
> The usual culprits: huge unoptimized hero images, too many third-party scripts (analytics, chat widgets, social embeds), and slow hosting.
>
> The fix is almost never "rebuild your whole site." It's compress the images, remove the third-party junk you don't need, and move to a host that's actually fast.

## Item 3 — Mobile-first, not just "mobile-friendly" (2:20–3:20)

> **Number three. Your site has to be mobile-first.**
>
> Around 65 percent of nonprofit web traffic comes from a phone now. For some causes — especially under-35 donors and crisis-driven giving — it's over 80 percent.
>
> "Mobile-friendly" just means your site doesn't break on a phone. "Mobile-first" means the *donation experience* is designed for a phone — big tap targets, no horizontal scrolling, Apple Pay and Google Pay enabled on the donation form.
>
> Open your own site on your phone right now. Try to donate $25. Time it. If it takes more than 30 seconds — including loading — you're losing donors.

## Item 4 — WCAG-level accessibility (3:20–4:20)

> **Number four. Accessibility.**
>
> This is not just legal compliance — though for many nonprofits it is also that. It's whether someone with low vision, color blindness, or a motor impairment can use your site to donate, register for an event, or read about your program.
>
> The basics:
>
> - Contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 on body text. (Free contrast checker: webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker.)
> - Alt text on every meaningful image.
> - Forms that work with a keyboard, not just a mouse.
> - Logical heading order — H1, then H2, then H3 — not jumping around for stylistic reasons.
>
> Tools like WAVE — wave.webaim.org — will scan your site and tell you what's broken. Free.
>
> Accessibility done right also makes your site rank better in Google. Same content, more channels reaching it.

## Item 5 — One email capture, not five (4:20–5:20)

> **Number five. One email capture.**
>
> Most nonprofit sites have three email signup forms on the homepage, a popup, a footer signup, *and* a chatbot asking for an email. That's not five chances to capture — that's confusing the visitor and depressing conversion on all five.
>
> Pick one moment in your site experience that earns the ask — usually right after a value-delivering piece of content, or at the bottom of an impact story. Put one good form there. Make the value exchange clear: "Get our quarterly impact report" beats "Sign up for our newsletter."
>
> Wire it to your CRM — Mailchimp, Fluent CRM, MailerLite, whatever you use. If signups don't auto-flow into a welcome sequence, you're collecting emails into a void.

## Item 6 — Analytics wired in (5:20–6:20)

> **Number six. Analytics, configured to actually track donations.**
>
> Most nonprofits have Google Analytics installed but never set up donation goal tracking. So they can tell you traffic is up, but they cannot tell you whether last month's email campaign drove $4,000 in donations or zero.
>
> Set up Google Analytics 4 (GA4). Configure a "donation completed" event. Tag your email campaigns with UTM parameters so you can see which campaigns convert. Set up Google Search Console so you know which queries bring people to your site.
>
> If your current site agency hasn't done any of this for you — that's a sign you need a different partner.

## Item 7 — Schema markup for nonprofits (6:20–7:00)

> **Number seven. Schema markup.**
>
> This is invisible code that tells Google "we are a nonprofit, here's our mission, here are our programs, here are our FAQs." Properly marked-up sites get richer search results — your FAQs can show up directly in Google, your program names get linked, your nonprofit gets the little "non-profit organization" tag.
>
> If you're on WordPress, plugins like Rank Math or Yoast handle the basics. For nonprofit-specific schema — NGO, FAQ, Service — you may need custom code. (That's part of what our `csuite-schema` plugin handles on our own site, and on client sites.)
