By Anthony Colasante, Founder, CSuite Code

Most small-business breaches exploit basic gaps - no multi-factor authentication, unpatched software, untested backups - not exotic hacks. This 15-point checklist covers the IT security fundamentals every business in Philadelphia, South Jersey, and Delaware should have in place in 2026. If you cannot confidently check off all 15, that is your roadmap.

Why does a small business need an IT security checklist?

Small businesses are targeted precisely because they are assumed to have weak defenses. Roughly 43% of cyberattacks target small businesses, and a large share of those that suffer a major breach struggle to recover. The good news: the controls that stop the overwhelming majority of attacks are well-understood, affordable, and on this list.

The 15-point small-business IT security checklist

Identity and access (1-4)

  1. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere. Email, banking, and every critical app. This single control blocks the vast majority of account-takeover attempts.
  2. A password manager for the whole team. Unique, strong passwords per account, no sticky notes or reused logins.
  3. Least-privilege access. Staff get access only to what they need; admin rights are rare and deliberate.
  4. Prompt offboarding. Accounts are disabled the day someone leaves, not weeks later.

Devices and software (5-8)

  1. Automatic patching. Operating systems and applications update on a schedule, not whenever someone remembers.
  2. Endpoint protection (EDR). Modern detection-and-response on every device, not just consumer antivirus.
  3. Full-disk encryption. On every laptop, so a stolen device is not a data breach.
  4. No unsupported systems. Anything past end-of-life (old Windows, unpatched servers) is replaced or isolated.

Data and continuity (9-12)

  1. Automated, encrypted backups. Following the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, two media, one off-site.
  2. Tested recovery. You have actually restored from backup - an untested backup is a guess, not a safety net.
  3. Email security and filtering. Anti-phishing, spoofing protection, and link scanning, since email is the top attack vector.
  4. A written incident response plan. Who to call and what to do, decided before an incident, not during one.

People and compliance (13-15)

  1. Security awareness training. Staff can recognize phishing and social engineering - your people are the real perimeter.
  2. Compliance mapping. If you handle health data (HIPAA), card data (PCI), or client financial data, your controls are mapped to those requirements.
  3. Regular review. The whole list is revisited at least annually, because your business and the threats both change.

How many of these does the average small business have?

In our experience auditing tri-state small businesses, most have four to eight of these fifteen in place when we first talk - usually antivirus and some backups, rarely MFA everywhere, tested recovery, or a written incident plan. The gaps are almost always in the controls that matter most during an actual attack.

What should you do first?

If you are starting from scratch, prioritize in this order: MFA everywhere (1), tested backups (9-10), endpoint protection (6), and email security (11). Those four stop or contain the large majority of incidents that actually hit small businesses. Everything else on the list hardens you further, but those are the load-bearing controls.
